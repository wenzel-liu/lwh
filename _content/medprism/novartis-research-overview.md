**Introduction:**

Novartis has undergone a strategic transformation in recent years to become a pure-play innovative medicines company. The company now focuses its Research & Development (R&D) on five core therapeutic areas – **Cardiovascular, Renal & Metabolic diseases; Immunology; Neuroscience; Solid Tumors; and Hematology** – where high unmet medical needs and strong growth potential align. This report provides a detailed overview of Novartis’ R&D focus areas (with special emphasis on cardiology), its drug pipeline across all stages of development, and an analysis of major clinical trials from the past five years. It also examines the geographical scope of these trials (notably in the US, EU, Japan, and China) and looks ahead to future trials and trends based on pipeline progress and regulatory plans. All information is current as of early 2025, and references are included for verification.

## 1. R&D Focus Areas

Novartis concentrates its R&D on five **core therapeutic areas**, reflecting a prioritization of resources into fields where it has significant expertise and where disease burden is high. In addition, Novartis continues to explore innovative platforms (like gene & cell therapies, radioligand therapy, and RNA-based treatments) that span these disease areas. Below is an overview of each R&D focus area, with a special focus on the **Cardiovascular** domain:

- **Cardiovascular, Renal & Metabolic Diseases (CRM):** This is a major R&D pillar for Novartis, addressing conditions like heart failure, atherosclerosis, kidney diseases, and metabolic disorders. Cardiovascular and metabolic diseases account for an enormous global health burden (nearly 18 million deaths annually) and remain an area of unmet need. Under the leadership of Dr. Shaun Coughlin, Novartis’s CRM research team pursues a diverse portfolio of approaches to prevent, slow, or reverse these diseases. Key **cardiology sub-focuses** include:
    - *Atherosclerosis:* Developing therapies to aggressively lower LDL cholesterol and reduce vascular inflammation, aiming to prevent heart attacks and strokes. Novartis’s pipeline in this area includes agents like **inclisiran (Leqvio)**, a small-interfering RNA that cuts LDL levels by ~50% with twice-yearly dosing, and **pelacarsen (TQJ230)**, an antisense oligonucleotide targeting lipoprotein(a) to address a risk factor not modifiable by diet or statins. Strategies to **reverse plaque buildup** are also under exploration. Notably, Novartis licensed pelacarsen from Ionis/Akcea in 2019 and launched the large Phase 3 Lp(a) HORIZON trial to prove it can reduce cardiovascular events.
    - *Heart Failure:* Building on the success of **Entresto® (sacubitril/valsartan)** in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), Novartis is investigating next-generation therapies for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and other forms of cardiac dysfunction. The company seeks to elucidate mechanisms of HFpEF (where the heart’s pumping chamber becomes stiff) and develop novel treatments. Entresto itself was evaluated in HFpEF in the PARAGON-HF trial, which in 2019 narrowly missed its primary endpoint but suggested benefits in certain subgroups. Novartis continues to study Entresto’s role in broader heart failure populations and combine it with other therapies to extend its benefits.
    - *Arrhythmias (Atrial Fibrillation):* Research is underway to find safer anti-arrhythmic drugs and potentially disease-modifying treatments for atrial fibrillation. Additionally, Novartis recently reinforced its cardiovascular portfolio by acquiring **abelacimab**, a novel Factor XI inhibitor anticoagulant, aimed at stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation without the bleeding risk of traditional blood thinners. Abelacimab is in Phase 3 trials (e.g., the LILAC-TIMI 76 study) for atrial fibrillation and could transform care for heart rhythm patients by reducing clot risk with fewer side effects.
    - *Kidney & Metabolic Diseases:* Because cardiovascular risk is closely tied to kidney function and metabolic health, Novartis’s CRM unit also targets **renal diseases** like IgA nephropathy and C3 glomerulopathy, and metabolic conditions like type 2 diabetes and obesity. Novartis’s first-in-class oral complement inhibitor **iptacopan (Fabhalta®)** originated from this research – it was *discovered at Novartis* and in 2023 became the **first of their renal pipeline** to achieve FDA approval. Iptacopan was initially approved for the rare blood disorder **PNH (paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria)** and has since shown promise in kidney diseases: a Phase 3 trial in **IgA nephropathy** met its interim goal by significantly reducing proteinuria, leading to an FDA **accelerated approval** in late 2024 for IgAN. Further Phase 3 studies are ongoing to confirm long-term renal benefits by 2025. Novartis is also exploring treatments for *obesity-driven diseases* and has stated an aim to find therapies that produce substantial, durable weight loss to mitigate diabetes and cardiovascular risk.
- **Immunology & Dermatology:** Novartis has a strong legacy in immunology, with successful drugs like **Cosentyx® (secukinumab)** for psoriasis/arthritis and **Xolair® (omalizumab)** for asthma. The R&D in this area spans inflammatory and autoimmune disorders of the joints, skin, and organs. Current focus includes: expanding *Cosentyx* into new indications (e.g., **giant cell arteritis** and **polymyalgia rheumatica**, both in Phase 3), and advancing novel agents for systemic autoimmune diseases. One key pipeline asset is **ianalumab (VAY736)**, a BAFF-receptor targeting antibody being tested in multiple autoimmune conditions: lupus nephritis, Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and systemic sclerosis. Ianalumab is in Phase 3 trials for Sjögren’s and lupus, aiming to become a first-in-class therapy in these conditions by modulating B-cell activity. Another major program is **remibrutinib (LOU064)**, an oral BTK inhibitor. Remibrutinib is in late-stage trials for **chronic spontaneous urticaria** (Phase 3 results in 2024 showed sustained efficacy and a favorable safety profile) and in Phase 3 for **relapsing multiple sclerosis** (trials ongoing, readouts expected by 2025). This drug could offer a new oral option for diseases where current treatments are injectable or have tolerability issues. Novartis is also pioneering the use of CAR-T cell therapy in autoimmune diseases: for example, **YTB323 (rapcabtagene autoleucel)**, a CD19-directed CAR-T originally developed for cancer, is being investigated in refractory **lupus nephritis/SLE** and dermatomyositis (Phase 2) to induce durable remissions by resetting the immune system. These efforts highlight Novartis’s innovative approach to immunology, extending beyond traditional biologics into cellular therapy and precision targeting of immune pathways.
- **Neuroscience:** In neuroscience, Novartis focuses on neurological disorders with high unmet need such as multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromuscular diseases, and rare neurodegenerative conditions. A flagship success is **Kesimpta® (ofatumumab)**, an anti-CD20 therapy for MS, and the company continues to build on this foundation. A major R&D thrust is in **spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)**: Novartis markets **Zolgensma®**, a one-time gene therapy for infants with SMA Type 1, and it has now developed an **intrathecal formulation (OAV101)** to treat older children with Type 2 SMA. In late 2024, the pivotal **Phase 3 STEER trial** of OAV101 in SMA Type 2 met its primary endpoint, showing significant motor function improvement in patients dosed via intrathecal administration. OAV101 is the *first investigational gene therapy to provide benefit in treatment-naïve SMA patients aged 2 and older*, and Novartis plans to file these results with regulators in 2025 to expand SMA treatment to this broader group. In neurodegenerative disease, Novartis has invested in **Huntington’s disease** research – for instance, in 2023 it in-licensed **PTC518**, an oral huntingtin-lowering agent (an mRNA splice modulator), now in Phase 2 development, after a prior Huntington’s candidate was discontinued. The company is also exploring therapies for neurological immune disorders (e.g., remibrutinib in MS as noted) and neuropsychiatric conditions via exploratory research units. Overall, the neuroscience pipeline is being bolstered by cutting-edge modalities such as gene therapy and RNA-targeted treatments to tackle diseases like SMA, MS, and Huntington’s that were previously intractable.
- **Oncology (Solid Tumors):** Oncology is a cornerstone of Novartis R&D, now refined to focus on select solid tumor types and advanced technology platforms. In solid tumors, Novartis emphasizes targeted therapies and **radioligand therapies (RLT)**. The company has defined a strategy around five priority tumor types and has pruned projects that don’t fit this focus. Key areas include breast cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and tumors amenable to targeted radiotherapy. Novartis has scored significant wins in oncology R&D:
    - In **breast cancer**, its CDK4/6 inhibitor **Kisqali® (ribociclib)** has become a standard in metastatic HR+/HER2- breast cancer, and in 2023 the Phase 3 **NATALEE trial** proved Kisqali can also benefit patients with early-stage (stage II/III) HR+/HER2- breast cancer. NATALEE was stopped early after Kisqali *plus endocrine therapy* showed a significant reduction in the risk of cancer recurrence compared to endocrine therapy alone. This marks the first positive trial for any CDK4/6 inhibitor in such a broad early breast cancer population, and regulatory submissions worldwide followed, pointing to an expanded adjuvant indication that could help tens of thousands of patients.
    - In **prostate cancer**, Novartis is pioneering radioligand therapy. It developed **Pluvicto™ (^[177]Lu-PSMA-617)**, a radioactive ligand targeting PSMA on prostate cancer cells. Pluvicto was first approved in 2022 for late-stage metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) after chemotherapy, based on the positive Phase 3 *VISION* trial. More recently, the Phase 3 **PSMAfore trial** showed that Pluvicto can provide a *highly significant benefit* earlier in the disease course (in mCRPC patients before chemotherapy). In PSMAfore, Pluvicto more than doubled median radiographic progression-free survival (12.0 months vs ~5.6 months on standard care), with a hazard ratio of 0.41 for progression risk. Although overall survival data are still maturing (crossover of control patients to Pluvicto complicates analysis), the rPFS benefit led Novartis to plan regulatory filings to move Pluvicto into the pre-chemotherapy setting. Beyond lutetium-177 beta emitters, Novartis is already developing next-generation **alpha-emitter RLTs**: for example, *Actinium-225-PSMA-617* and a second-gen *Ac-PSMA-R2*, which are in Phase 1-2 and soon entering Phase 2/3 trials in 2025. These alpha therapies could potentially deliver more potent cell-killing to tumors and are part of Novartis’s strategy to remain at the forefront of radiopharmaceuticals.
    - In **lung cancer** and other solid tumors, Novartis has honed its efforts on targeted pathways. The company had explored KRAS inhibition (with a G12C inhibitor **JDQ443**), but discontinued it in 2023 amid strategic pipeline pruning, likely due to a competitive landscape. Instead, Novartis is leveraging combination approaches (such as SHP2 inhibitors to enhance KRAS targeting) and investing in external innovation (e.g., its 2022 acquisition of *Mariana Oncology* to access preclinical alpha-particle therapies for lung cancer). Other notable solid tumor projects include **TNO155** (a SHP2 inhibitor in trials for RAS/MAPK-driven cancers) and immunotherapy combinations, although Novartis has deprioritized some immuno-oncology programs to focus on areas of strength.
    - The **radioligand platform** deserves special mention: Novartis is expanding RLT beyond prostate cancer into gastrointestinal tumors (*Lutathera®* targets neuroendocrine tumors and is being tested in earlier lines), and exploring RLT for breast, colon, and other cancers. The company significantly invested in RLT manufacturing, doubling Pluvicto production capacity by opening new facilities in 2023, and plans new sites in China and Japan to support global demand. This platform is a clear R&D priority for Novartis, combining its oncology and nuclear medicine expertise.
- **Hematology (Blood Cancers & Disorders):** Hematology is another core area, often managed alongside oncology but distinguished by diseases of the blood and bone marrow (like leukemias, lymphomas, sickle cell disease, etc.). Novartis has a legacy in hematology (e.g., therapies for CML and sickle cell) and continues to advance treatments for both malignant and non-malignant blood disorders. In **chronic myeloid leukemia (CML)**, Novartis introduced the first-in-class STAMP inhibitor **Scemblix® (asciminib)**, which targets the ABL myristoyl pocket. Initially approved in 2021 for refractory CML, Scemblix was tested as a first-line treatment in the Phase 3 **ASC4FIRST trial**. By 2023, 48-week data from ASC4FIRST were strong enough to garner FDA approval for Scemblix in newly diagnosed CML, making it an alternative to standard TKIs with a different mechanism. Extended 96-week results presented in late 2024 confirmed durable responses, solidifying Scemblix’s role in front-line CML therapy. In **lymphomas**, Novartis is developing next-generation CAR-T therapies: besides its marketed Kymriah® (tisagenlecleucel) for pediatric ALL and certain lymphomas, the pipeline includes **YTB323** (a faster, enhanced CAR-T process) being trialed in high-risk large B-cell lymphoma (Phase 2) to potentially improve on Kymriah’s manufacturing time or efficacy. In **immune-mediated hematologic diseases**, as noted under immunology, ianalumab is being repurposed for conditions like **immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)** and **warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia**, where B-cell modulation could help (Phase 3 trials are planned or ongoing for these indications). Novartis is also active in rare hematology: for example, **crizanlizumab** (an anti-P-selectin antibody) was approved for sickle cell pain crises, and they have gene therapy research (through alliances) for disorders like sickle cell and beta-thalassemia. Finally, hematology overlaps with complement-mediated diseases: iptacopan’s approval in **PNH** (a blood disorder) and ongoing trials in atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) highlight cross-portfolio synergy between hematology and renal R&D. In summary, Novartis’s hematology R&D is focused on innovative mechanisms (STAMP inhibitors, CAR-T, complement inhibitors) to address cancers of the blood and rare blood disorders.

**Additional R&D Domains:** While the five areas above are the main investment priorities, Novartis also maintains **Global Health and exploratory research programs**. These include developing medicines for diseases of the developing world and for regenerative medicine. For instance, the pipeline still features anti-malarial projects (e.g., novel agents like **cipargamin (KAE609)** and **ganaplacide (KLU156)** in Phase II/III for malaria) and a treatment for visceral leishmaniasis. These efforts are often done in partnership and underscore Novartis’s commitment to global health challenges beyond commercial markets. Furthermore, the company’s **“DAx” (Diseases of Aging and Exploratory) research unit** investigates nascent areas like regenerative medicine (e.g., cartilage repair – though a trial in osteoarthritis with LNA043 was discontinued) and other cutting-edge science that could feed into future pipelines.

Overall, Novartis’s R&D strategy is to *“focus on five core attractive therapeutic areas, key technology platforms, and the US market”* as stated by CEO Vas Narasimhan. The sharpened focus aims to boost R&D productivity and value per project, concentrating on high-impact medicines and leveraging new technologies to “reimagine medicine.” The special emphasis on cardiology and related metabolic/renal diseases is evident in the pipeline and recent investments, as Novartis seeks to address the world’s leading causes of death with innovative treatments.

## 2. Pipeline Coverage: Early, Mid, and Late-Stage Drugs

Novartis possesses a **deep and diverse pipeline** of investigational drugs, with around *136 clinical-stage assets* after a recent portfolio reprioritization. The pipeline spans all phases of development (Phase I to III and registration) and aligns with the aforementioned core therapeutic areas. In 2023, Novartis trimmed ~10% of its pipeline projects to concentrate resources on the most promising and strategic programs, ensuring each aligns with its five core areas and has strong commercial potential. Despite the cuts, the pipeline remains rich, with **over 100 ongoing clinical development projects**. Below is a comprehensive overview of the pipeline, organized by therapeutic area, highlighting key drugs in **early, mid, and late stages** of development:

### Cardiovascular, Renal & Metabolic Pipeline

Novartis’s CRM pipeline is robust, featuring multiple late-stage programs aimed at cardiovascular risk reduction and renal diseases, as well as earlier-stage innovations. Key pipeline candidates include:

- **Pelacarsen (TQJ230):** A first-in-class antisense oligonucleotide targeting apolipoprotein(a), designed to lower **lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]** levels. Pelacarsen is in a **Phase 3 outcomes trial (Lp(a) HORIZON)** enrolling patients with cardiovascular disease and elevated Lp(a). This trial, which began in 2019, aims to prove that lowering Lp(a) can reduce heart attacks and strokes; it is expected to complete by around mid-2025. If successful, pelacarsen could become the **first therapy for elevated Lp(a)**, addressing an inherited risk factor affecting 20–30% of CVD patients. Novartis obtained pelacarsen via a deal with Ionis/Akcea and exercised its option to license it in 2019, reflecting its commitment to “transformational” cardiac therapies. The **HORIZON trial’s outcome** will dictate regulatory filings; analysts view pelacarsen as a potential game-changer if it shows cardiovascular event reduction.
- **Inclisiran (Leqvio):** A small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy for hypercholesterolemia. Inclisiran is already approved in the EU (and in the US as of late 2021) for lowering LDL cholesterol in high-risk patients. It remains in the pipeline for expanded uses – notably, **outcomes studies and broader preventive indications**. Two major Phase 3 trials (ORION-10 and ORION-11) reported in 2019-2020 demonstrated that inclisiran, given twice a year, cut LDL-C by ~50% on top of statins. These results, published in *NEJM*, confirmed durable LDL reduction with a safety profile similar to placebo. Ongoing studies like ORION-4 (conducted with Oxford University) are assessing if inclisiran’s LDL lowering translates into fewer cardiovascular events (heart attacks, strokes). Additionally, Novartis is exploring inclisiran in **“primary prevention”** settings – the pipeline lists a project for primary prevention of cardiovascular risk (for patients without an event yet but high LDL) in Phase 3 with a planned filing ≥2028. By 2027, inclisiran is expected to be a cornerstone of Novartis’s cardiometabolic portfolio, potentially expanding the use of LDL-lowering therapy due to its convenient infrequent dosing.
- **Entresto (sacubitril/valsartan) and Next-Generation Heart Failure Therapies:** Entresto is an approved product rather than a pipeline drug, but Novartis continues to assess new indications and combinations for it (such as post-myocardial infarction heart failure, although the 2021 PARADISE-MI trial did not show a clear benefit in that setting). In parallel, the company’s pipeline includes **novel molecules for heart failure** (e.g., myosin modulators or alternative neurohormonal blockers). One known pipeline candidate was **XXB750** for hypertension, which has been discontinued in development, indicating a strategic move away from crowded mechanisms. However, Novartis has signaled interest in **heart failure with preserved EF** and could be evaluating agents that modulate fibrosis or inflammation in the heart (exact early-stage compounds are not public). Additionally, Novartis’s acquisition of Anthos brings in **abelacimab** (mentioned above under arrhythmias), which while primarily an anticoagulant, intersects with cardiovascular outcomes (stroke prevention). Abelacimab’s Phase 3 program (LILAC, MAGNOLIA, ASTER trials) will read out in 2025–2026, potentially leading to **regulatory filings for atrial fibrillation stroke prevention** if positive. This would add a late-stage asset to the CV portfolio in an area (anticoagulation) that complements heart failure and lipid management.
- **Renal Disease Assets (IgAN & C3G):** The pipeline is particularly strong in IgA nephropathy (IgAN), a chronic kidney disease that often progresses to renal failure. **Iptacopan (Fabhalta)** is at the forefront here: after its successful IgAN Phase 3 (APPLAUSE-IgAN) hit the proteinuria reduction endpoint, Novartis secured FDA priority review and in 2024 **obtained accelerated approval** for Fabhalta in IgAN. The full trial remains blinded until 24-month data on kidney function are collected by 2025, as required to confirm long-term benefit. Meanwhile, Novartis augmented its renal pipeline by **acquiring Chinook Therapeutics in 2023**, gaining two Phase 3 IgAN candidates: **atrasentan (renamed EXV811)** and **zigakibart (BION-1301, renamed FUB523)**. *Atrasentan* is an oral endothelin receptor antagonist; its Phase 3 trial (ALIGN) has shown positive interim results and Novartis is pursuing regulatory filings in the US – in fact, an NDA for atrasentan in IgAN has been or will be submitted around 2024 (pipeline status shows “US registration” for IgAN). *Zigakibart* is an anti-APRIL monoclonal antibody that targets a key driver of IgAN; it entered Phase 3 in late 2023. If successful, Novartis could potentially offer a **trio of complementary IgAN therapies** – iptacopan, atrasentan, and zigakibart – attacking the disease via different mechanisms. In **C3 glomerulopathy (C3G)**, another rare kidney disease, iptacopan is in Phase 3 (the APPEAR-C3G trial) and has reported some data. CEO Vas Narasimhan indicated optimism that Fabhalta (iptacopan) will have strong uptake in C3G as well, and Phase 3 results are anticipated soon. These renal programs underscore Novartis’s substantial pipeline commitment to kidney diseases within its cardiometabolic umbrella.
- **Other Cardiometabolic Pipeline Projects:** Novartis is investigating therapies for **obesity and NASH** (though none are publicly disclosed, the focus on “obesity-driven disease” suggests early-stage research in metabolic hormones or novel mechanisms). In diabetes, Novartis has fewer late-stage projects (given many competitors), but the cardiometabolic strategy is likely to incorporate combination regimens (for example, combining an SGLT2 inhibitor with Novartis’s heart failure or lipid drugs in trials). The company also continues work in **thrombosis** beyond abelacimab – it had a Factor XII inhibitor project (perhaps in preclinical) and has partnered efforts in anti-platelet therapies in the past. Given the pipeline pruning, any early-stage CV projects that didn’t show promise have been cut, allowing focus on those listed above. Notably, Novartis’s **canakinumab (IL-1β inhibitor)** – which was a big bet for preventing heart attacks (the CANTOS trial in 2017 showed efficacy in cutting CV events via inflammation reduction) – was repurposed into oncology (lung cancer trials that unfortunately failed in 2021). While canakinumab is no longer a CV pipeline asset, its legacy lives on in Novartis’s strategy of tackling inflammatory pathways in heart disease (e.g., via anti-IL-6 or other cytokine inhibitors in early research).

In summary, the cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic pipeline at Novartis is **late-stage heavy** with potentially transformational therapies (pelacarsen, inclisiran, iptacopan, atrasentan, abelacimab) that could come to market in 2024–2026, and it’s backed by early research into next-gen approaches for heart failure, arrhythmia, and metabolic diseases. This pipeline reflects Novartis’s aim to maintain leadership in cardiometabolic medicine by addressing cholesterol, lipoprotein(a), thrombosis, and kidney disease in novel ways.

### Immunology Pipeline

Novartis’s immunology pipeline builds on its strengths in inflammatory diseases and seeks to expand into new autoimmune conditions. It features both new molecules and new indications for existing drugs:

- **Cosentyx (secukinumab) – New Indications:** Cosentyx is an IL-17A inhibitor already approved for psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and axial spondyloarthritis. The pipeline extends Cosentyx into difficult-to-treat inflammatory diseases: **Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)** and **Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)**. Both are rheumatologic conditions with limited treatments (steroids are standard). Cosentyx is in Phase 3 for GCA (expected primary completion in 2024) and in Phase 3 for PMR (through 2025). If these trials succeed, Cosentyx could become the first steroid-sparing biologic for GCA/PMR, tapping into new patient populations. Another earlier exploratory use was **rotator cuff tendinopathy**, but that program was discontinued in development, possibly due to insufficient efficacy. Overall, Novartis aims to broaden Cosentyx’s reach as a platform immunology drug.
- **Ianalumab (VAY736):** As mentioned, ianalumab is a BAFF-R (B-cell activating factor receptor) inhibitor antibody in **Phase 3 trials for Sjögren’s syndrome and lupus nephritis/SLE**, and in Phase 2 for systemic sclerosis and myositis. It’s an important mid-to-late-stage asset, as no targeted biologic is yet approved for Sjögren’s (an autoimmune disease causing dry eyes/mouth and systemic issues) or for systemic lupus (beyond a few B-cell or interferon-targeting drugs). By depleting or modulating B-cells via BAFF-R, ianalumab could reduce autoantibody production and inflammation in these diseases. The pipeline listing suggests planned filings: e.g., for Sjögren’s by 2026 if Phase 3 is positive. Notably, ianalumab is also being studied in some hematology settings (first-line ITP, etc.) highlighting its cross-disciplinary potential. It represents Novartis’s **next-generation immunology** approach, succeeding older compounds like iscalimab (CFZ533, an anti-CD40, which was discontinued in Sjögren’s in favor of ianalumab).
- **Remibrutinib (LOU064):** A small-molecule Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor, remibrutinib is one of Novartis’s most advanced immunology assets. It is in **Phase 3 for Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria (CSU)** – a condition of frequent hives and itching, often inadequately controlled by antihistamines. In May 2024, Novartis announced that Phase 3 data confirmed remibrutinib’s sustained efficacy and long-term safety in CSU, positioning it as a potential oral alternative to Xolair (the injectable anti-IgE antibody) which is current standard for refractory cases. Two Phase 3 trials (REMIX-1 and -2) met their endpoints, and regulatory filings in chronic urticaria are expected in late 2024 or 2025. Additionally, remibrutinib is in **Phase 3 for Relapsing Multiple Sclerosis**, with two large studies (REMODEL) due to read out by 2025. This could put Novartis in a competitive spot in MS, offering an oral BTK inhibitor that penetrates the brain and potentially slows progression (a new mechanism beyond B-cell depletion and S1P modulation). Remibrutinib is also in early trials for other autoimmune diseases (the pipeline lists Myasthenia Gravis as a possible indication in Phase 3 starting 2024). Given its broad potential, analysts have dubbed remibrutinib a “pipeline in a pill,” and Novartis is likely to pursue multiple indications if initial approvals go well.
- **Pipeline Biologics in Allergy and Inflammation:** Novartis had developed **ligelizumab (QGE031)**, a next-gen anti-IgE antibody for allergies and urticaria, but in 2021 it failed to beat Xolair in Phase 3, leading to discontinuation. This setback shifted focus to remibrutinib for urticaria. Another interesting program was **Xolair in food allergy** – a large Phase 3 (OUTMATCH) combining Xolair with oral immunotherapy for peanut allergy was conducted. The pipeline list marked “Xolair – Food allergy: Commercialized”, suggesting the trial may have succeeded and Novartis (with partner Genentech) might be moving to file or at least consider the results for label expansion. (As of 2023, Xolair is not formally approved for food allergy, so “commercialized” likely indicates the program ended, possibly with a decision not to file or to use the data in other ways.) In severe asthma, Novartis’s anti-TSLP antibody **tezepelumab** (developed by Amgen/AstraZeneca) is already approved, but Novartis markets it in some regions – that’s more a product than pipeline.
- **Innovative Cell and Gene Therapies:** Beyond traditional biologics, Novartis’s immunology pipeline ventures into cell therapy (as noted, **YTB323 CAR-T** for lupus/SLE is in Phase 2 after a proof-of-concept where CAR-T therapy induced drug-free remission in lupus patients – a groundbreaking approach). If this works, it could revolutionize treatment for refractory autoimmune diseases by effectively “rebooting” the immune system. Additionally, Novartis’s exploratory research includes **regenerative medicine** for autoimmune or fibrotic diseases (e.g., programs to regenerate damaged tissue in systemic sclerosis or osteoarthritis, though one such molecule, LNA043 for cartilage repair, was discontinued). Gene therapy isn’t a major feature in immunology except indirectly (e.g., *Zolgensma* for a neurogenetic disease, or potential future gene therapies for conditions like protein deficiencies).

Novartis’s immunology pipeline is notable for its **breadth** – spanning oral small molecules, monoclonal antibodies, and cell therapy – and for targeting diseases ranging from common (e.g., urticaria, MS) to rare (e.g., Sjögren’s). The late-stage prospects like ianalumab and remibrutinib could significantly expand Novartis’s immunology franchise in the next 2-3 years, while mid-stage projects like the CAR-T in lupus show a willingness to pursue high-risk, high-reward innovations.

### Neuroscience Pipeline

The neuroscience pipeline at Novartis is evolving, with an emphasis on neuromuscular diseases, multiple sclerosis, and neurodegeneration:

- **OAV101 (Intrathecal Onasemnogene Abeparvovec):** This is the **next wave of Novartis’s SMA gene therapy**. OAV101 is essentially Zolgensma adapted for spinal delivery, allowing treatment of older or larger patients who cannot receive the one-time intravenous infusion (which is limited to infants due to dosing and antibody considerations). The Phase 3 **STEER trial** in SMA Type 2 (patients aged 2 to 18 who can sit but not walk) read out positively in late 2024: it met the primary endpoint, demonstrating a significant improvement in motor function (HFMSE score increase) in children treated with a single **intrathecal injection** of OAV101 compared to sham control. Safety was favorable and comparable to control. These results are a milestone – OAV101 is the first gene therapy to show benefit in treatment-naïve older SMA patients. Novartis plans to submit this data to regulatory agencies in 2025, aiming to **expand SMA gene therapy access** to a broader patient population. If approved, it could markedly increase the number of patients who can benefit from a one-time gene replacement (currently, older SMA patients rely on chronic drugs like nusinersen or risdiplam). This program also validates Novartis’s gene therapy platform and supports continued investment in gene delivery for neurological diseases.
- **Multiple Sclerosis:** In addition to the marketed drugs Gilenya (fingolimod) and Kesimpta, the pipeline’s main MS project is **remibrutinib**, as discussed, which is in Phase 3. Another MS-related pipeline asset was **branaplam**, an oral splicing modifier originally for SMA, which Novartis repurposed for Huntington’s disease and possibly considered for MS. However, branaplam’s development in Huntington’s was halted in 2022 due to side effects (peripheral neuropathy). To fill this gap, Novartis licensed **PTC518** in 2023, a small molecule that lowers mutant huntingtin protein by modifying splicing of its mRNA (similar concept as branaplam but potentially safer). PTC518 is in Phase 2 for Huntington’s disease currently. While not yet in Phase 3, if it shows promise, it could advance and become a notable neuroscience asset – Huntington’s has no disease-modifying treatments yet, so a successful trial would be high-impact. For MS, aside from remibrutinib, Novartis’s ongoing interest might include neuroprotective approaches (e.g., alpha synuclein or Parkinson’s research via partnerships, although nothing major is public in late-stage pipeline).
- **Neuropsychiatry and Pain:** Novartis historically had some psychiatric pipeline (e.g., compounds for schizophrenia or autism), but in the current focus, these are deprioritized. Pain research might be part of their “exploratory” projects (such as Nav1.7 blockers for chronic pain, which some big pharmas pursue) – but again, nothing high-profile has been disclosed post-2022 reorganization.
- **Ophthalmology/Neuro-ophthalmology:** Novartis’s neuroscience area sometimes overlaps with ophthalmology (for example, **Beovu® (brolucizumab)** is an eye drug for wet AMD, which is more of a global health category or separate from the five core areas since Novartis did not highlight ophthalmology in its core list). However, Beovu is in the pipeline for **diabetic retinopathy** (Phase 3 completed and filing in 2024). Additionally, a gene therapy for an eye disorder (like an inherited retinal dystrophy) was in earlier research, but the main pipeline in eye diseases is currently Beovu’s new use and perhaps gene therapy collaborations (Novartis had one with Voyager for a retina-targeted capsid, but that’s preclinical). These are tangential to neuroscience but worth noting as part of the broader pipeline.

In summary, the neuroscience pipeline is anchored by the SMA gene therapy expansion and MS programs, with some targeted efforts in rare neuro diseases (Huntington’s) and leveraging gene therapy/RNA tech. As neurological drug development is challenging, Novartis’s strategy here seems to combine leveraging proven modalities (like Zolgensma) in new populations and cautiously advancing high-potential new mechanisms for diseases like Huntington’s. The success of OAV101 will likely influence future neuroscience pipeline investments (possibly encouraging more gene therapy for other neurological conditions).

### Oncology & Hematology Pipeline

Novartis’s oncology pipeline (covering solid tumors and hematologic malignancies) is deep, though recently streamlined to prioritize high-value assets. Key components of the pipeline:

- **Kisqali (ribociclib) – Early Breast Cancer:** Having met its endpoint in the NATALEE trial for adjuvant HR+ breast cancer, Kisqali’s label expansion is imminent. In pipeline terms, this program is essentially complete (“commercialized” as of 2023) and awaiting approvals. Once approved, it will move fully out of pipeline into marketed status. The success of NATALEE will make Kisqali one of the first CDK4/6 inhibitors approved in the curative early breast cancer setting, which could significantly boost its usage. Follow-up studies and real-world research will likely continue (e.g., assessing long-term outcomes, combination with other adjuvant therapies), but the pivotal development is done.
- **Radioligand Therapies (Pluvicto and Next-Gen RLTs):** Pluvicto (^[177]Lu-PSMA-617) is approved for late-line prostate cancer and showed positive Phase 3 results in earlier-line mCRPC (PSMAfore) as discussed. Novartis is preparing regulatory filings in H1 2024 for **pre-chemotherapy mCRPC** based on PSMAfore, potentially leading to an approval by H1 2025. In parallel, **Lutathera** (^[177]Lu-DOTATATE) for neuroendocrine tumors is being tested in first-line G2/G3 GEP-NETs (Phase 3) to broaden its use. The exciting pipeline additions are the **actinium-225 labeled RLTs**:
    - *Ac-PSMA-617* (first-generation alpha emitter targeting PSMA) – an **Phase 2/3 trial (PSMAction)** is planned to start in April 2025 for patients who have already received lutetium therapy (post-Pluvicto progression). This trial will first refine dosing and then assess efficacy vs. standard care in a post-lutetium setting, effectively trying to extend the radioligand approach to those who exhaust beta therapy.
    - *Ac-PSMA-R2* (second-generation alpha PSMA radioligand) – currently in **Phase 1/2 (the “Satisfaction” study)**, aimed at mCRPC patients earlier in the line of therapy (including some who never had Pluvicto). This agent might have different tumor targeting properties.
    - Beyond prostate cancer, Novartis has alpha-RLT programs from its acquisition of Endocyte and Advanced Accelerator Applications, and the 2022 purchase of **Mariana Oncology** gave it a pipeline of preclinical RLTs (e.g., an actinium-225 conjugate for small cell lung cancer). Also, Novartis is investigating RLTs for **other cancers (breast, colon, etc.)** by linking radioisotopes to relevant tumor-targeting molecules. These are mostly in discovery or Phase 1.
        
        Radioligand therapy thus constitutes a significant part of Novartis’s oncology pipeline strategy, with multiple ongoing trials that could yield new products in the next 3-5 years and keep Novartis ahead in this modality.
        
- **Targeted Therapies in Solid Tumors:** Novartis continues to advance targeted small molecules and biologics, particularly in *lung cancer*. They had two notable lung cancer targeted agents:
    - **Tabrecta® (capmatinib)** – a MET inhibitor for MET-mutated NSCLC (approved in 2020, co-developed with Incyte). It’s marketed, but further studies (like combinations with immunotherapy) could be considered pipeline activities.
    - **JDQ443** – a KRAS G12C inhibitor. This was in Phase 2, but as part of pipeline refocusing, Novartis opted to discontinue JDQ443 in 2023. Likely, the decision came because Amgen’s and Mirati’s KRAS inhibitors were already on market and Novartis was late to the game, plus the field is shifting to next-gen KRAS targeting (like pan-KRAS or KRAS G12D), so Novartis may redirect efforts there or exit that race.
        
        Another asset is **TNO155** (SHP2 inhibitor) in Phase 1/2, which can be combined with KRAS inhibitors or other agents to prevent tumor escape mechanisms. TNO155 studies are ongoing with partners. In melanoma or solid tumors with MAPK pathway issues, Novartis has **LXH254**, a RAF dimer inhibitor in Phase 2 trials, sometimes combined with MEK or other pathway blockers. There’s also an oncolytic virus (viral therapy) program from a collaboration (with Microsoft for AI-designed viruses, or Dyno for capsid, but those might be early).
        
        In summary, targeted therapy pipeline in solid tumors is relatively thinner after pruning; Novartis seems to favor investments where it can be first or best in class (e.g., their focus on radioligands, or on breast cancer CDK4/6 leadership, rather than me-too TKIs).
        
- **Cell Therapy and Immuno-oncology:** Novartis was a pioneer with Kymriah CAR-T. Pipeline efforts continue to improve CAR-T technology:
    - **YTB323** (rapid CAR-T) and related programs (like PHE885, a BCMA CAR-T for myeloma using the same T-Charge platform) are in development. YTB323 for large B-cell lymphoma 1st line (high-risk patients) is in Phase 2, aiming to move CAR-T earlier in treatment, potentially replacing stem cell transplant if successful.
    - There’s a BCMA CAR-T for multiple myeloma (from Novartis’s platform) entering Phase 3, to compete with approved CAR-Ts from BMS and J&J – though Novartis hasn’t heavily publicized it yet, it’s known as *cilta-cel* competitor.
    - On the T cell engager front, Novartis had some programs (e.g., **talquetamab** for myeloma in-licensed from Xencor, though J&J moved faster with their own). Not much is publicly listed, possibly these are early or shelved.
    - In checkpoint inhibitors, Novartis notably *does not have its own PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor* in development (it licensed tislelizumab from BeiGene for some regions, but that’s more for commercial use rather than R&D). Instead, it’s exploring next-gen immunotherapies like **TIM-3 inhibitor (sabatolimab)** for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and AML. *Sabatolimab* was in Phase 3 (STIMULUS trials) for high-risk MDS; however, in mid-2023 Novartis announced that sabatolimab did not meet the Phase 3 primary endpoint in MDS. Further development is uncertain, and this highlights the risk in immunotherapy for myeloid malignancies.
    - Novartis is also dabbling in NK cell engagers or macrophage targets. For example, it had an anti-CD47 (to make cancer cells more “visible” to macrophages) – an agent called **TTI-622** partnered with Trillium/Pfizer; not sure if Novartis is still pursuing that after Trillium’s acquisition by Pfizer.
- **Hematologic Malignancies:** Apart from CML (Scemblix) and lymphoma (CAR-Ts), Novartis’s pipeline includes a **BET inhibitor (pelabresib)** for myelofibrosis, which came with the 2020 acquisition of MorphoSys’s assets (via a deal involving Monjuvi). *Pelabresib (KES) also known as CPI-0610*, now **pelabresib (DAK5392)**, is in **Phase 3 (MANIFEST-2)** for first-line myelofibrosis, in combination with ruxolitinib. It’s intended to improve on JAK inhibitor therapy by modifying gene transcription in malignant cells. This is a high-value pipeline asset in hematology—if it significantly boosts responses in myelofibrosis, it could be a blockbuster. The pipeline table shows it entered confirmatory development in 2024, likely reflecting the start of Phase 3 after positive Phase 2 data.
    
    In acute leukemias, Novartis had some early programs (e.g., invenetoclax combos, or targeted agents like Xospata is from Astellas but Novartis had an FLT3? Not known). They also have a partnership for a next-gen BCR-ABL inhibitor (with Molecular Partners for a DARPin targeting ABL? But asciminib largely covers that need).
    
- **Other Notable Pipeline Removals:** As part of transparency, Novartis listed projects removed in 2023: **LNA043** (for osteoarthritis) discontinued, **CFZ533** (anti-CD40 for transplant/Sjögren’s) discontinued, **JDQ443** (KRAS G12C) discontinued, **QGE031** (ligelizumab) discontinued, **XXB750** (antihypertensive) discontinued. These decisions reflect an effort to concentrate on pipeline drugs that are either first-in-class or best-in-class in the core areas. For example, discontinuing ligelizumab after it didn’t surpass Xolair freed resources for remibrutinib in urticaria, and dropping JDQ443 allows focus on other oncology avenues like radioligands. The pipeline “pruning” has thus shaped the current slate of drugs toward those with clear differentiating potential.

In conclusion, Novartis’s pipeline across early, mid, and late stages is well-aligned with its strategic focus areas. Late-stage programs (Phase III/registration) like Kisqali (adjuvant BC), pelacarsen, iptacopan, atrasentan, remibrutinib (CSU/MS), Scemblix (1L CML), Pluvicto (earlier mCRPC), Lutathera (earlier NET), and pelabresib (myelofibrosis) are poised to drive growth if approvals are obtained. Mid-stage programs (Phase II) like novel CAR-Ts, PTC518 for Huntington’s, alpha-emitter RLTs, and ianalumab in additional diseases ensure a next wave behind them. Early-stage research (Phase I and preclinical) is feeding innovative platforms, as seen with gene therapies and exploratory disease research, to sustain the pipeline long-term.

Notably, Novartis is leveraging **technology platforms** across these areas – for instance, *xRNA (siRNA/ASO)* technology in inclisiran and pelacarsen, *gene therapy* in SMA, *cell therapy* in CAR-T for lupus and cancer, and *radioligands* in oncology. Over 50 projects in exploratory or early clinical development are tied to these new platforms. This structured yet diverse pipeline is intended to produce a steady stream of high-impact new molecular entities (NMEs) in Novartis’s core therapeutic domains.

## 3. Clinical Trials Overview (2019–2024)

Over the past five years, Novartis has conducted numerous clinical trials, several of which have been high-impact, practice-changing studies. This section highlights **major clinical trials from 2019 through 2024**, particularly those that resulted in significant data readouts, approvals, or new directions for Novartis. The focus is on *pivotal Phase II/III trials* in Novartis’s core areas, especially cardiology (as requested), but also including landmark trials in oncology, immunology, etc. We also note trials across key geographies (many of these are global trials with sites in the US, EU, Japan, China, etc.). Here are some of the **key clinical trials and their outcomes** in the last five years:

- **2019: PARAGON-HF (Heart Failure, Entresto)** – This Phase III trial evaluated **Entresto** in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (**HFpEF**). While PARAGON-HF *just missed* its primary endpoint (p=0.06) for reducing cardiovascular death and HF hospitalizations in HFpEF overall, it showed a 13% relative risk reduction and suggested benefits in certain subgroups (notably in women and patients with mid-range ejection fraction). The trial’s nuanced outcome was *high-impact* because HFpEF had no proven therapy; as a result, regulators expanded Entresto’s indication to include HFpEF in 2021 (based on totality of evidence and subgroup analyses). PARAGON-HF spurred further HFpEF research and underscored the heterogeneity of this condition. It was a globally conducted study (sites across North America, Europe, Asia, including Japan) and its data have influenced treatment guidelines to cautiously recommend sacubitril/valsartan in select HFpEF patients.
- **2019: EXSCEL & CANTOS Follow-ups (Cardiology)** – In 2019 Novartis also assessed canakinumab’s potential in cardiovascular prevention after the CANTOS trial (2017) had shown a 15% reduction in CV events by an anti-inflammatory approach. However, by 2019, Novartis decided not to pursue canakinumab in heart disease (instead launching the CANOPY program in lung cancer, covered below). Meanwhile, Novartis’s partner-driven trials like **EXSCEL** (with AstraZeneca on a GLP-1 agonist) were more peripheral to its own pipeline. The big cardiology pivot in 2019 was the **pelacarsen program** initiation: Novartis formally announced the plan for the **Lp(a) HORIZON outcomes trial** in Feb 2019, a significant commitment to a new kind of therapy. That trial (randomizing ~8,000 patients to pelacarsen vs placebo) has been ongoing since late 2019 and represents a potential paradigm shift if positive (no results yet; expected 2025).
- **2020: ORION-9, -10, -11 (Inclisiran LDL-C Trials)** – Three Phase III trials of **inclisiran** reported full results, with two major ones published in *NEJM* in March 2020. **ORION-10** (United States) and **ORION-11** (Europe and South Africa) tested inclisiran in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or risk equivalents who had elevated LDL despite maximal statin therapy. **ORION-9** tested it in familial hypercholesterolemia. All three trials met primary endpoints: at 17 months, inclisiran lowered LDL-C by ~50% compared to placebo. The time-adjusted LDL reduction over 18 months was similarly ~50%. Importantly, inclisiran was given every 6 months by subcutaneous injection in a doctor’s office, showing the feasibility of infrequent dosing. The safety profile was comparable to placebo (no major liver or muscle issues beyond what statins cause). These trials were high-impact as they supported regulatory approval; the FDA and EMA reviews in 2020-2021 used these data. Inclisiran got EU approval in late 2020 and eventual FDA approval in Dec 2021. The trials had broad geographical reach (ORION-11 notably included EU sites and South Africa, ORION-10 was US-focused, ORION-9 had sites in multiple countries), demonstrating Novartis’s global trial footprint. The NEJM publication and accompanying editorials discussed how inclisiran could improve long-term adherence for cholesterol management by shifting to a semi-annual model.
- **2020: MONALEESA-7 and -3 (Kisqali in Breast Cancer)** – Though earlier MONALEESA trials for Kisqali (ribociclib) in metastatic breast cancer reported results in 2017-2018, **updated overall survival data** were presented in 2020 (ESMO 2020) showing significant OS benefit for Kisqali in HR+ metastatic breast cancer (especially premenopausal women, MONALEESA-7). This solidified Kisqali’s profile and influenced its use. These were not new trials launched but continued follow-up from prior pivotal trials; nonetheless, they were high-impact publications in 2020, keeping Novartis at the forefront of the CDK4/6 inhibitor field and setting the stage for exploring early BC (the NATALEE trial was initiated around 2018 and was ongoing by 2020).
- **2020: CANOPY-1 and CANOPY-2 (Canakinumab in Lung Cancer)** – Novartis attempted to repurpose the anti-IL1β drug **canakinumab** for oncology, based on the observation in CANTOS that it reduced lung cancer incidence and mortality. They launched **CANOPY-1** (canakinumab + chemo + Keytruda in first-line NSCLC) and **CANOPY-2** (canakinumab + docetaxel in second-line NSCLC). These trials were underway in 2019-2020. In 2021, Novartis announced that CANOPY-2 failed to improve overall survival in second-line lung cancer, and in 2022 CANOPY-1 also did not meet its endpoint. While not successful, these trials were notable in Novartis’s recent clinical portfolio as high-profile experiments linking inflammation to cancer. They underscore that not all major trials yield positive results, but even negative outcomes guided Novartis to reallocate efforts (post-2022, Novartis has de-emphasized canakinumab and immuno-oncology in favor of other areas).
- **2021: VISION (Pluvicto in mCRPC)** – The Phase III **VISION trial** was a landmark for Novartis’s radioligand therapy. Conducted in 2018-2020 with results revealed in 2021, VISION tested **[^177]Lu-PSMA-617 (Pluvicto)** plus standard care vs. standard care alone in men with PSMA-positive metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who had progressed after chemo and ARP inhibitors. VISION met both primary endpoints: it significantly prolonged radiographic PFS and overall survival. Median overall survival was improved by 4 months (15.3 vs 11.3 months; HR 0.62), and rPFS by an even greater margin. The results, published in *NEJM* in 2021, were practice-changing, leading to **FDA approval of Pluvicto in March 2022**. This trial was high-impact not only for Novartis but for oncology, as it validated a new treatment modality (targeted radiotherapy) in a solid tumor. VISION was global (175 sites in North America, EU, Asia, including in China and Japan as part of bridging studies). It set the stage for further Pluvicto trials (like PSMAfore for earlier stage, which began before VISION read out, and others).
- **2021: UltIMMa and PEARL (Ligelizumab in Urticaria)** – Two Phase 3 trials called PEARL 1 and 2 evaluated **ligelizumab** (QGE031) vs Xolair in chronic spontaneous urticaria. In late 2021, Novartis reported these did *not* show superiority of ligelizumab over Xolair, failing to meet the primary endpoint. This was a notable trial result in immunology; as a consequence, Novartis halted development of ligelizumab. While a disappointment, it was a high-impact event as it re-routed the urticaria program towards remibrutinib. The trials themselves were large (each ~ ~800 patients, across 40+ countries including US, EU, Asia) and their outcome helped refine Novartis’s strategy (sometimes knowing what *not* to pursue is as impactful as a success).
- **2022: APPOINT-PNH and APPLY-PNH (Iptacopan in PNH)** – Two Phase 3 trials for **iptacopan** in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) reported in 2022. **APPOINT-PNH** studied iptacopan in PNH patients not on complement inhibitor therapy, and **APPLY-PNH** in patients switching from standard C5 inhibitors (like eculizumab). Both trials met endpoints: a good proportion of patients on iptacopan achieved transfusion independence and improved hemoglobin levels. These results led to regulatory submissions, and in **December 2023 the FDA approved iptacopan (Fabhalta) for PNH**, making it the first oral alternative to injectable C5 inhibitors. These trials were pivotal for bringing Fabhalta to market. PNH is a rare disease, so trials were smaller and multinational (sites in US, EU, Asia). The success in PNH also provided confidence for using iptacopan in kidney diseases (which, as noted, saw success in 2023 with IgAN interim results).
- **2022: CANOPY-1 (Canakinumab in 1L NSCLC)** – Final readout was negative (no improvement in overall survival for the addition of canakinumab to immunotherapy/chemo). As a result, Novartis terminated further canakinumab oncology trials. This freed up R&D resources and taught a valuable scientific lesson about the role of IL-1β in cancer.
- **2022: LUCENT-1 and -2 (Remibrutinib in Chronic Urticaria)** – Phase 2b trial LUCENT-1 for remibrutinib in CSU showed dose-dependent efficacy in reducing hives and itch, encouraging Novartis to move to Phase 3. By late 2022, Phase 3 trials (REMIX) were fully enrolled. Though the main Phase 3 readouts came in 2024, the progression of these trials in 2022 was a key part of Novartis’s clinical development, aiming to fill the gap left by ligelizumab.
- **2023: NATALEE (Kisqali in Early Breast Cancer)** – This Phase 3 trial was one of the **biggest oncology stories of 2023**. NATALEE tested 3 years of **Kisqali + endocrine therapy** vs endocrine therapy alone in **HR+/HER2- early breast cancer** patients (stage II and III) including node-negative patients – a broader population than prior adjuvant CDK4/6 trials. In March 2023, an interim analysis showed **Kisqali significantly cut the risk of invasive disease recurrence**, meeting the primary endpoint so convincingly that the independent data monitoring committee recommended stopping the trial early for efficacy. Kisqali plus hormonal therapy showed consistent benefit regardless of nodal status. This led to filings (submitted in 2023) and by Q4 2023, some approvals: notably, in **October 2023 the EU approved Kisqali for adjuvant treatment** based on NATALEE (making it the first CDK4/6 inhibitor approved in that setting). The US and other regions are reviewing it in 2024. NATALEE’s success is high-impact as it will expand the patient pool for CDK4/6 inhibition to earlier-stage cancer, potentially improving cure rates. The trial was global with significant U.S., European, and Asian participation (to reflect diverse patient populations). It bolsters Novartis’s oncology portfolio and could drive multi-billion dollar sales for Kisqali.
- **2023: ASC4FIRST (Asciminib in First-line CML)** – Another big result in hematology: the Phase 3 **ASC4FIRST** trial evaluated **Scemblix (asciminib)** as initial therapy in chronic-phase CML, versus the standard-of-care TKIs (imatinib or others). By 2023, 48-week data showed superior efficacy of asciminib, and with a favorable safety profile, the FDA granted approval for **first-line use of Scemblix in Ph+ CML-CP** (announced in October 2023). Longer 96-week results presented at ASH 2024 reinforced the benefit. This trial is significant because it may establish asciminib as a new first-line option, particularly beneficial for patients who cannot tolerate or do not optimally respond to existing TKIs. The trial was widely conducted (CML trials typically include centers in North America, Europe, and Asia given CML’s global incidence). By achieving first-line approval, Novartis further capitalized on an internally discovered drug, extending the legacy of its CML franchise (from imatinib to nilotinib to asciminib).
- **2023: PSMAfore (Pluvicto in earlier mCRPC)** – As detailed, PSMAfore trial results were unveiled at ESMO 2023. This trial met its endpoint, showing Pluvicto can benefit patients earlier in prostate cancer (pre-chemotherapy). The high crossover in the trial (84% of control arm got Pluvicto after progression) complicated OS analysis, but the rPFS and quality-of-life gains were clear. This data was high-impact, as it will likely lead to Pluvicto label expansion in 2024/25, moving RLT up in treatment lines and thus affecting how oncologists sequence therapies for prostate cancer. For Novartis, positive PSMAfore results also validate continued investment in RLTs and upcoming alpha emitter trials (since it shows that targeting PSMA earlier still yields benefit).
- **2023: APPLAUSE-IgAN (Iptacopan in IgA Nephropathy)** – Novartis announced in late 2023 that the Phase 3 APPLAUSE trial of **iptacopan in IgAN** met its interim endpoint with a significant reduction in proteinuria. In early 2024, detailed results were shared: a 38% greater reduction in urine protein for iptacopan vs placebo at 9 months. The FDA granted priority review and, in **September 2024, approved iptacopan (Fabhalta) under accelerated approval for IgAN**. This trial is particularly important as it positions Novartis in the renal rare disease space and showcases the role of complement inhibition in IgAN. The trial will continue to assess long-term renal outcomes (eGFR decline) through 2025. APPLAUSE-IgAN was global (sites in North America, Europe, Asia-Pacific including Japan, and China as well). The outcome also influenced Novartis’s business development – seeing positive data, they acquired Chinook to augment their IgAN portfolio.
- **2024: REMIX-1 & 2 (Remibrutinib in Chronic Urticaria)** – In early 2024, Novartis disclosed that the Phase 3 trials of **remibrutinib in chronic spontaneous urticaria** met their primary endpoints, with remibrutinib providing significant relief from hives and itch versus placebo, and a favorable safety profile in long-term (96-week) extension. These results, coming after years of development, were highly anticipated. They set the stage for regulatory filings (likely in 2024) for what could be the first BTK inhibitor for an allergic condition. If approved, remibrutinib could offer a convenient oral therapy for patients uncontrolled on antihistamines, potentially replacing or delaying the use of Xolair injections. This is a high-impact trial for immunology, as it introduces a new mechanism (BTK inhibition) for treating a condition traditionally managed by targeting IgE. It also validates BTK as a target in other IgE-mediated or autoantibody conditions, hinting at broader use (e.g., in food allergy or other mast cell disorders, though those would require separate studies).
- **2024: STEER (OAV101 Intrathecal in SMA Type 2)** – Completed in late 2024 with positive results (as discussed in the neuroscience section). This is high-impact as it opens up gene therapy to a new patient group and could change the standard of care for SMA Type 2 (currently Spinraza or Evrysdi). It’s one of the first trials to show gene therapy can work in an older pediatric population for a neurodegenerative disease. Novartis will likely present full data at a neurology conference in 2025 and submit for approvals.
- **2024: Other Notable Ongoing Trials:** A few trials spanning 2019-2024 haven’t reported yet but are worth noting as they approach completion:
    - **HORIZON (Pelacarsen in CVD):** Ongoing since 2019, finishing in 2025, as noted. If it reads out positive (reduction in MACE events), it will be one of the most impactful trials of the decade for cardiology, validating a novel risk factor. If negative, it will also be highly informative for the field.
    - **ORION-4 (Inclisiran outcomes):** Run by Oxford University, expected around 2026 – will inform whether LDL lowering via siRNA yields similar event reduction as statins/PCSK9 antibodies.
    - **NX-VERITAS (Novartis’s next-gen CAR-T vs standard CAR-T in lymphoma):** Could be any ongoing comparison for YTB323 vs tisagenlecleucel, etc., results perhaps 2024-25.
    - **Lutetium-PSMA in earlier disease:** Novartis might be running a trial in hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (given pipeline lists one in metastatic hormone-sensitive setting by 2025). That might be in Phase 3 currently (PSMAddition maybe).
    - **Tislelizumab combination trials:** Novartis in-licensed tislelizumab (PD-1) from BeiGene for some markets; there were trials combining tislelizumab with Novartis’s own agents (like in liver cancer with Yttrium-90, etc.). While not core to R&D focus, any positive outcome could lead to filings (though tislelizumab is more a commercial strategy in EU/US since it’s approved in China).
    - **Myelofibrosis (pelabresib):** Ongoing Phase 3, results maybe 2025.
    - **Solid tumor early combos:** For example, Novartis has a Phase 2 called **BELLE** combining alpelisib (PI3Kα inhibitor) with other agents in various tumors; not high-impact yet but part of their trial portfolio.

Overall, **clinical trials in the past five years** for Novartis have delivered multiple new approvals and label expansions:

- Cardiovascular: Entresto HFpEF (label expanded 2021), inclisiran (approved 2020 EU/2021 US), upcoming pelacarsen and abelacimab results.
- Renal: Iptacopan (PNH 2023, IgAN 2024 approvals).
- Oncology: Pluvicto (approved 2022), Kisqali adjuvant (approval 2023-24), Scemblix 1L (2023), new radioligand trials initiated.
- Immunology: Remibrutinib success (2024), Cosentyx likely success in GCA (data not public yet, but if positive could be 2024-25), failure of ligelizumab (2021) but pivot to other approaches.
- Neuroscience: SMA gene therapy extension (2024).
- Hematology: CAR-T in lupus (small trials published in 2022 in Lancet, led to pipeline trial in 2023), and classical hem malignancies like CML (asciminib) and MDS (sabatolimab, albeit negative).

These trials have been conducted across **key geographies**. For instance, large Phase III programs almost always include North America (US/Canada) and Europe (EU countries). Japan often participates in global trials or has parallel “JP-specific” trials to support local registration; Novartis typically includes Japan in its global studies for major drugs (e.g., Japan sites were in PARAGON-HF, in ORION trials, in oncology trials like MONALEESA and likely NATALEE, etc.). China’s role in trials has grown: Novartis, like others, has increased trial activity in China and even aimed to be a “top 3 player in China” by leveraging both global trials and some China-only bridging studies. Indeed, Novartis has run China-specific studies (for example, testing drugs in Chinese patient subsets for local approval) and is building R&D capacity there. Over 2019-2024, several Novartis trials had Chinese sites – including big ones like ORION-11, some Cosentyx trials, and certainly Pluvicto’s development (since China approval is a goal, they plan a manufacturing site there). The company’s **“US-first” trial mindset** instituted in 2022 means the US has the most representation in trials and often earlier enrollment, but other regions remain crucial for patient recruitment and regulatory requirements.

To summarize, Novartis’s recent clinical trial track record shows a mix of **successes (leading to new therapies)** and some **setbacks (informing pipeline refocus)**. The high-impact trials mentioned above have largely strengthened Novartis’s position in its core areas: e.g., Entresto and Leqvio in cardiology, Pluvicto and Kisqali in oncology, asciminib in CML, and iptacopan in renal disease. These trials, conducted worldwide (US, EU, Japan, China and beyond), have paved the way for the next generation of treatments and shaped the current pipeline.

## 4. Geographical Scope of Trials (US, EU, Japan, China)

Novartis is a global company, and its R&D and clinical trials reflect a broad geographical scope. However, strategic priorities have shifted to emphasize certain regions:

- **United States (US):** The US is now the primary focus for Novartis’s clinical development and market strategy. In recent years, Novartis adopted a **“US-first” approach** to trials and regulatory filings, aiming to increase the share of US patients in its clinical studies and to seek FDA approvals early. This pivot is driven by the goal for Novartis to become a top-5 player in the US market by 2027. Practically, this means many pivotal trials are designed with FDA input and often have significant US enrollment. For example, trials like ORION-10 (inclisiran) were US-specific, and others like NATALEE and PSMAfore had substantial US patient representation. Novartis has also concentrated investment in US R&D infrastructure, such as expanding manufacturing for radioligand therapies in New Jersey to supply Pluvicto to trial sites and patients. The US-first mindset is evident in the swift US filings for drugs like Kisqali (adjuvant) and iptacopan, and in **Phase 3 trial placement** – many programs now ensure they include enough US sites to support robust FDA submissions. Additionally, Novartis’s **Meetings with FDA** (e.g., breakthrough therapy or fast track designations like abelacimab’s Fast Track in 2022) shape trial design to meet US standards. In summary, the US is not only a major trial locale but often the pace-setter for Novartis’s global drug development.
- **Europe (EU):** Europe has traditionally been a strong base for Novartis trials (with the company’s headquarters in Basel and major R&D centers in Switzerland and Italy). Many pivotal studies have large European cohorts – e.g., ORION-11 was largely in Europe, and trials like PARAGON-HF and ASC4FIRST had many European sites. The EU remains critical for regulatory approval (EMA) and commercial success, but in trials Novartis may sometimes take FDA-first, EMA-second approach in timing. Still, the company maintains a significant European trial footprint. Countries like Germany (one of Novartis’s key markets) are often among top recruiters in trials, and Novartis aims to maintain a leading position in Germany. The EU is also where Novartis sometimes runs **unique studies** due to local needs – for example, the **European HIT trials** for TKI discontinuation in CML or certain pediatric trials might be centered in EU academic networks. Post-Brexit, Novartis manages UK trials separately but continues strong UK presence (e.g., significant trials run with Oxford in UK like ORION-4). In essence, EU participation in Novartis trials ensures diversity and generalizability of data to different healthcare systems, and many novel therapies (like inclisiran, which was co-developed via the UK’s NHS in a population study) involve European partnerships.
- **Japan:** Japan is a top pharmaceutical market and Novartis prioritizes it for both trials and early access. The company aims to maintain a leading position in Japan. Many global trials include Japan, but sometimes Japan runs parallel local Phase 1 or bridging studies due to ethnic difference considerations or regulatory practice. For instance, for gene therapies like Zolgensma or CAR-T, Japan often requires local data – Novartis conducted a Japanese trial of Zolgensma before approval there. For small molecules and antibodies, Japan often joins global Phase 3. In the Kisqali NATALEE trial, Japanese patients were included (or a separate subgroup study was done) to support approval in Japan soon after the West. Novartis also collaborates with Japanese research groups (e.g., some oncology trials are done with participation of Japanese cooperative groups). Recognizing the importance of Japan, Novartis is investing in local RLT production (planning a facility in Japan for radioligand therapies), which will support both trial supply and eventual commercial supply. In the past five years, Japan saw approvals of key Novartis drugs like Entresto, Cosentyx, and Kisqali in similar timeframes to US/EU, thanks to inclusion in trials or dedicated Japanese trials. Future pipeline drugs (pelacarsen, remibrutinib, etc.) will likely have Japanese bridging studies if needed to ensure Japan approvals soon after global ones.
- **China:** China has emerged as a *priority growth market* for Novartis. The company explicitly aims to be a top-3 player in China by 2030, which has led to increasing integration of China in its R&D endeavors. Historically, China had separate development pathways, but more recently Novartis has moved to include China in global trials or conduct China-specific Phase 3 studies in parallel. For example, Novartis ran China trials for inclisiran and Entresto to get those approved in China relatively quickly. They have also localized production (a planned radioligand manufacturing site in China suggests they foresee a need for clinical and commercial RLT supply in China). Regulatory reforms in China have made it easier to use foreign data; thus, Chinese patients in global trials mean simultaneous NDA submissions can happen. Novartis has increased Chinese patient enrollment – e.g., the ORION-11 trial included South Africa and could have had some China sites, but more directly, Novartis sometimes runs *China extension studies*. By 2022, Novartis said it’s focusing on “priority geographies including China” for growth, and part of that is by doing **development programs aligned with Chinese regulators’ priorities** (like trials for chronic hepatitis B cure – Novartis had some early programs there – or for gastric cancer, which is prevalent in Asia). In the last five years, notable Chinese trial activities include: a partnership with Chinese companies (e.g., licensing in tislelizumab for China/global), contributing Chinese patients to the CANOPY trials and others, and launching **local post-marketing studies** to satisfy China’s conditional approvals (like for Zolgensma, which got approval in China in 2022, Novartis likely has to do a registry or safety study locally). Going forward, China will feature even more in Novartis’s clinical trial plans as it reforms to accept global trial data and as Novartis increases investment there.
- **Other Regions:** While the question emphasizes US, EU, Japan, China, it’s worth noting Novartis runs trials truly globally. Emerging markets like Brazil, India, and countries in Eastern Europe have been part of trial networks. Novartis also conducts trials in Australia/New Zealand, often for early phase oncology (due to speed and quality of research there). In certain diseases, specific regions dominate (for example, Chagas cardiomyopathy trials might run in Latin America, malaria trials in Africa – Novartis’s malaria drug trials had many African sites). In gene therapy, Novartis collaborated with centers in **Eastern Europe** for SMA (some of the pivotal Zolgensma data came from a trial in Poland). For rare diseases, Novartis often includes **global patient communities** through travel to trial centers.

In summary, Novartis’s trial geography strategy is **global with regional focus**: pivotal trials are usually multi-continental, ensuring data for US, EU, Japan, and increasingly China approvals. The company’s strategy update in 2022 made it clear that *growth is expected to come by winning in the US and China*, while sustaining strength in Europe and Japan. This is being implemented by:

- Prioritizing US patient enrollment and FDA interactions (to potentially make the US the first filing country for key drugs).
- Heavily involving China (via either inclusion in globals or quick follow-on studies) to narrow the lag between Western and Chinese drug approvals.
- Keeping Japan and Germany (as an EU proxy) as important benchmarks – ensuring trials meet their regulatory needs so that approvals there are smooth.
- Harmonizing development such that one global trial can support approvals across all major regions whenever possible, to avoid redundant studies.

Novartis’s global trial operations have allowed simultaneous submissions: for instance, the company often announces FDA and EMA filings around the same time (as seen with iptacopan for PNH and IgAN, or Kisqali NATALEE data being submitted worldwide). This is a testament to their geographically inclusive trial design.

## 5. Future Clinical Trials and Trends

Looking ahead, Novartis’s pipeline and recent regulatory moves provide insight into the **future clinical trials** the company will be conducting, as well as broader R&D trends. Publicly disclosed future trials, along with anticipated data readouts and regulatory filings, suggest that the next few years (2025 and beyond) will see Novartis doubling down on its core areas with a few noticeable trends:

- **Cardiovascular Outcomes Trials Coming to Fruition:** The cardiovascular pipeline will reach critical milestones. The **Lp(a) HORIZON trial** for pelacarsen (started 2019) is expected to report results in 2025. If positive, Novartis will likely rapidly file for approval of pelacarsen in major markets (perhaps seeking **fast-track or breakthrough** designation, given the unmet need for Lp(a) lowering). A positive HORIZON could also spawn follow-up studies (e.g., in specific subpopulations like patients with aortic stenosis driven by Lp(a), etc.). Additionally, Novartis has initiated or will initiate **outcome trials for inclisiran** in broader prevention (ORION-4 completes around 2026, but an interim or another trial might read out earlier for secondary endpoints). The trend is that Novartis is investing in *hard outcome evidence* for its cardiometabolic drugs to solidify their roles.
- **Next-Gen Antithrombotics (Factor XI inhibitors):** With the acquisition of Anthos Therapeutics announced in Feb 2025, Novartis inherits ongoing Phase 3 trials of **abelacimab**. The lead trial **LILAC-TIMI 76** in atrial fibrillation will likely read out in 2025 or 2026. Two other trials, **ASTER and MAGNOLIA**, for cancer-associated thrombosis, are also underway. Novartis will integrate these into its development plans. We anticipate **future trials** if abelacimab succeeds: for example, comparing abelacimab to direct oral anticoagulants in broader AF populations, or testing it in orthopedic surgery thromboprophylaxis. The trend is moving toward safer anticoagulation (Factor XI inhibition) as a new standard – Novartis is positioning to be at the forefront of that if abelacimab’s trials are positive. Expect **regulatory filings** perhaps in 2026 for the AF indication, assuming data are favorable.
- **Expanding Heart Failure and Obesity Efforts:** While not as public as some programs, Novartis’s focus on heart failure (especially HFpEF) and obesity-related diseases implies future trials in those domains. For HFpEF, Novartis might test novel compounds (if any in pipeline) or combinations like Entresto + empagliflozin (just an example) to see incremental benefits. For obesity, if Novartis has a candidate (for example, a long-acting GLP-1 or a combination therapy), we would expect Phase 2 trials in obesity or NASH to start. One known upcoming trial is **ENTRUST-Beta** (hypothetical name) – if Novartis chooses to combine an SGLT2 inhibitor with Entresto in acute HF or something, but no specific details yet. The emphasis in statements on “substantial and durable weight loss” suggests Novartis may either partner (maybe with a company like Science in obesity) or have an internal GLP-1 co-agonist (though nothing is public, it’s an area to watch for announcements).
- **Immunology and Dermatology Trials:** As current late-stage trials finish (e.g., remibrutinib CSU done, ianalumab in Sjögren’s ongoing), Novartis will likely initiate new studies to broaden these drugs:
    - **Remibrutinib in other indications:** We can expect **future Phase 3 trials of remibrutinib in inducible urticaria (e.g., cold-induced urticaria)** since Phase 2 results were promising, and possibly in **food allergy or atopic dermatitis** (areas where BTK may play a role in mast cell or B-cell mediated pathology). Indeed, the pipeline lists “Chronic inducible urticaria” for remibrutinib with a planned filing in 2026, so those trials will conclude around 2025. If CSU approval comes, Novartis might quickly move to start trials in **adjacent allergic conditions**, capitalizing on a class lead.
    - **Ianalumab in SLE and renal lupus:** As Phase 3 in Sjögren’s and lupus nephritis progresses, Novartis might also trial ianalumab in **other autoimmune niches**. For example, a trial in **cutaneous lupus** or **rheumatoid arthritis** could be contemplated if BAFF-R blockade shows broad immunomodulation. Another future aspect is if ianalumab shows strong results in Sjögren’s, combination trials (with, say, an anti-T cell agent) might be explored to try for higher remission rates. These would be post-2025 actions likely.
    - **Cosentyx new indication follow-ups:** If Cosentyx Phase 3 in GCA (Giant Cell Arteritis) is positive, a filing in 2025 will follow. Similarly for PMR in 2026. The trend is extending the life cycle of Cosentyx. We may see **future trials of Cosentyx in other autoimmune diseases** like hidradenitis suppurativa or asthma (though Novartis hasn’t signaled those yet, IL-17 is being explored in airway disease by others).
    - **Emerging Immunology Targets:** Novartis might initiate trials for novel targets from its labs or in-licensing. For instance, IL-15 or IL-7 pathway inhibitors for autoimmune disease, or perhaps an anti-IFN for Type I interferonopathies. Also, **DCST** (plasmacytoid dendritic cell depletion) for cutaneous lupus was an area they once looked at (BIIB059 was taken by Biogen though). So watch for any new MoA entering Phase 1 in 2025.
- **Neuroscience Trials and Neurotech:** With gene therapy for SMA moving to filing, Novartis could pursue **gene therapy trials for other neuromuscular diseases**. They have a collaboration with Genethon for a gene therapy in **Limb Girdle Muscular Dystrophy** (this might enter clinical trial in the near future). They also have interest in ALS (Novartis partnered with gene therapy for SOD1 ALS from AveXis which didn’t pan out). We might see **Novartis join forces on a gene therapy for Friedreich’s ataxia or Duchenne** (speculative, but those areas are hot and Novartis has the platform). In Huntington’s, with PTC518 in Phase 2, if that shows even moderate efficacy in lowering mutant huntingtin and clinical outcomes, Novartis could expand into a Phase 3 around 2025/26, which would be a major future trial to watch. In MS, if remibrutinib succeeds, **long-term extension and head-to-head trials vs existing MS drugs** may be planned to solidify positioning (for instance, a trial vs. Aubagio or vs. Ocrevus to show comparative efficacy or safety). Additionally, Novartis’s purchase of **Gyroscope Therapeutics** (if happened? Actually Gyroscope was acquired by Novartis in 2021 for an eye gene therapy for geographic atrophy – that trial (HORIZON, coincidentally same name) is ongoing Phase 2 for an AAV gene therapy in eye). That is more ophthalmology, but future trial wise, a **Phase 3 in geographic atrophy** for GT005 (Gyroscope’s gene therapy) could start under Novartis in 2025 if Phase 2 data are good. So, Novartis’s future trials might quietly include **eye disease gene therapy** as well, broadening the neuroscience/ophthalmology portfolio.

    - The Phase 2/3 **PSMAction trial (Ac-PSMA-617)** is slated for **April 2025 start** per a clinicaltrials.gov entry. This is a significant future trial – it will gather dose-finding data then transition into Phase 3 to evaluate efficacy of the actinium-225 PSMA therapy in mCRPC patients who have had Pluvicto. If it shows strong activity and manageable safety, it could support a first approval of an alpha emitter in prostate cancer around 2027.
    - The Phase 1/2 **Satisfaction trial (Ac-PSMA-R2)** will continue to dose-escalate and likely expand to define the best indications – by 2025 or 2026, if data are good, Novartis might skip to Phase 3 or pivot resources to whichever of the two alpha agents looks superior.

Actually, Novartis did a deal with Clovis for FAP-targeted RLT FAP-2286). If promising, this could move to Phase 3 for cancers like pancreatic or ovarian by 2026.

    - In targeted therapies: **Lung cancer** – although JDQ443 is out, Novartis still invests in **MET exon14 (Tabrecta)** expansions and **EGFR** (it had nazartinib for EGFR mutant but discontinued in favor of EGF816 which also got discontinued). Instead, Novartis might join the chase for **KRAS G12D** or **SOS1 inhibitors** (some big pharmas are doing that). If they do, those would be early trials not yet public.
    - **Myelofibrosis (pelabresib)**: The Phase 3 will finish ~2025. If positive, a filing and possible approval by 2026. If that happens, **post-approval trials** will follow (like combo of pelabresib + another new agent). Possibly, Novartis could test pelabresib in **acute myeloid leukemia** or other malignancies (BET inhibitors have broad epigenetic effects).
    - **New IO Trials:** Novartis might not emphasize PD-1, but it is working on next-gen immune therapies. For instance, they had a **STING agonist** in trials (failed due to tox), and a **TLR7/8 agonist** (which might still be in Phase 1). Future trials might include combination of those with a checkpoint inhibitor in solid tumors. Also, Novartis licensed a **LAG-3 antibody (LAG525)** and **TIM-3 (sabatolimab)** – LAG-3 (leramilimab) could be in a Phase 3 with PD-1 (though competitor BMS already got a LAG-3 approved). If Novartis finds a niche like LAG-3 in liver cancer or TIM-3 in combination for AML maintenance, they might do new Phase 3s accordingly, but this is uncertain given some disappointments.
- **Cell/Gene Therapy Trials:** After SMA, Novartis’s gene therapy pipeline includes:
    - **OAV101 IV in SMA Type 1 expansion** – actually done (the STR1VE trial was done when Zolgensma was first approved). The future is more about line extensions: possibly testing OAV101 in combination with another therapy (Spinraza?) – unlikely, since one-time gene therapy stands alone usually.
    - **CAR-T for Autoimmune**: If the Phase 2 with YTB323 in lupus shows good results around 2025, Novartis could launch a **larger trial or a pivotal Phase 3 in lupus** to seek approval of CAR-T for autoimmune disease (a groundbreaking endeavor). That would be a future trial to watch – perhaps a multinational trial in refractory SLE patients with YTB323 or similar.
    - **Gene editing therapies:** Novartis hasn’t publicly fronted a CRISPR trial, but it has research collaborations. Possibly by 2025-26, it may enter a partnership trial for a CRISPR-based therapy (e.g., for sickle cell or beta-thalassemia, competing with CRISPR Therapeutics/Vertex or Bluebird/Beam efforts). If so, it would join an already competitive area.
- **Regulatory Filings Driving Trials:** The cadence of **regulatory filings** often dictates post-marketing trials. For instance:
    - **Kisqali Adjuvant:** Post-approval, authorities might require a follow-up safety/efficacy study in certain populations (or an open-label extension to track long-term outcomes beyond 5 years). We might see a **survivorship trial** or registry.
    - **Iptacopan IgAN accelerated approval** means Novartis *must complete* the full trial to confirm benefit by 2025. If the confirmatory data (eGFR slope) are positive, the drug stays; if not, they might need another trial or risk withdrawal. So far trends are good.
    - **Fabhalta in C3G**: If Phase 3 data in C3G look good (as hinted they have some data), Novartis may file for accelerated approval in C3G as well, which would involve new trials or extensions (like a long-term C3G outcomes trial perhaps required).
    - **Pluvicto earlier use filing (pre-chemo)** in 2024 might come with a commitment to complete OS follow-up in PSMAfore or do a **real-world study** to confirm benefit.
    - **Remibrutinib filing in urticaria** (if accelerated or conditional in some regions) may require an additional trial in a broader population or longer duration.
- **Emerging Trends Based on Pipeline:**
    - A clear trend is **convergence of therapeutic areas with technology platforms**. Novartis is exploiting its platforms across diseases: e.g., using *siRNA/ASO (xRNA)* for both cardio (inclisiran, pelacarsen) and potentially other areas (they might apply ASO to a rare disease next); using *gene therapy* beyond SMA (maybe for eye or blood disorders next); *CAR-T* beyond cancer (in autoimmune). Therefore, we might see *platform trials* or new indications where a known technology is applied to a different disease (like a CAR-T trial in a different autoimmune disease, or an ASO trial in a genetic cardiomyopathy).
    - **Precision medicine and biomarkers:** Future trials will likely incorporate biomarker-defined populations. For instance, if pelacarsen works, Novartis might only target patients with Lp(a) > a certain threshold. Similarly, radioligand trials require biomarker (PSMA-PET positivity). Cosentyx in GCA requires biomarker (imaging) of vascular inflammation etc. The trend is using diagnostics to enrich trials – Novartis has a partnership with tech companies to improve trial patient finding (they’ve mentioned AI in R&D, possibly to stratify who enters a trial).
    - **Digital Trials and Decentralization:** Not specifically asked, but industry-wide, there is a trend for decentralized trials. Novartis has been experimenting with digital tools (like using sensor data in some MS trials, or remote monitoring in heart trials). By 2025, more Novartis trials might allow remote visits, especially after the pandemic accelerated this trend.
- **Publicly disclosed future trials:** Some specifics we know:
    - Novartis has disclosed it will present or has started **over 65 abstracts** at ASH 2024, meaning many ongoing trials in hematology will share updates (like new combinations in leukemia or longer follow-ups of CARTITUDE etc.). While not all are future trials, this level of activity suggests momentum continuing.
    - **PTC518 Phase 2 results** (Huntington’s) in 2025 may determine if a Phase 3 trial starts in 2025/26. Novartis would then disclose a Phase 3 plan if it goes ahead.
    - The **EFGARTIGIMOD (VYVGART) competitor**: Novartis had interest in neonatal Fc receptor blockers for IgG-mediated diseases (Argenx has Vyvgart). Novartis tested one (fragment) but nothing major. If they re-enter, that’s a trial area – speculative.
    - **In-house BTK inhibitor for oncology (LOXO-305 competitor)**: Maybe they have or will in-license one for lymphomas – could lead to a new trial.

To encapsulate trends: **Novartis is streamlining to fewer, larger trials focused on high-value outcomes**. The days of numerous exploratory Phase 2s with marginal improvements are being replaced by either big bets (like pelacarsen outcomes) or paradigm shifts (CAR-T in autoimmune, gene therapy in new ages, alpha emitters). We will see **fewer but more impactful trials**. The company also emphasizes **operational excellence in trials**, implying faster recruitment and better execution. For example, Novartis touted that integration within R&D will help accelerate development – meaning future trials might reach readouts faster or with more efficiency (e.g., the NATALEE trial was run in about 3.5 years from start to interim result, which is quick for adjuvant setting).

From a regulatory angle, Novartis has several **expected approvals in 2024-2025** that result from recent trials:

- **Kisqali (adjuvant)** – likely approved globally by 2024/25.
- **Scemblix (1L CML)** – recently approved US, likely EU/Japan approvals in 2024.
- **Iptacopan (IgAN)** – accelerated approved US (Dec 2024), full approval after confirmatory data (likely 2025/26); EU decision in 2025 likely.
- **Leqvio (inclisiran)** – already in EU, in US under use via FDA approval, but an outcomes claim might be added after ORION-4 in future.
- **Pluvicto (pre-chemo)** – regulatory decision in H1 2025 (per Novartis, expecting outcome by then).
- **Pelacarsen** – if HORIZON is positive, filings late 2025 into 2026, potential approval in 2026.
- **Remibrutinib (CSU)** – filings 2024, approvals maybe 2025.
- **Abelacimab** – if acquired in H1 2025, and assuming data late 2025/26, possibly a filing ~2026.
- **OAV101 IT (SMA type 2)** – filings 2025, possible approval late 2025 or 2026.
- **Atrasentan (IgAN)** – likely US filing in 2024 (since pipeline said “US registration”), could get approved 2025 if priority review (especially since others like sparsentan got approved).
- **Zigakibart (anti-APRIL)** – still in Phase 3, likely result ~2026, filing beyond that.

**Future trial trends based on these**: Once these are approved, **Phase IV studies or new combination trials** will spawn. For example, after pelacarsen, maybe trial of pelacarsen + PCSK9 inhibitor vs either alone to see if dual LDL + Lp(a) lowering is additive for risk reduction. Or combining iptacopan with existing IgAN therapy (steroids or sparsentan) to see if dual therapy better protects kidneys.

Another trend is **deeper penetration into China**: likely **Novartis will conduct local Phase 3 or Phase 4 studies in China** for drugs like Kisqali adjuvant (Chinese subset), inclisiran outcomes (maybe a China outcomes study mandated), and novel assets like TQJ230 (which is probably including Chinese patients but if not, they might do a China-only trial post-global to support NRDL listing).

In conclusion, the future of Novartis’s clinical trials will be characterized by:

- A focus on **large, outcome-driven trials** in cardiovascular and renal medicine (to demonstrate real clinical benefits beyond surrogate markers).
- Continued **expansion of indications** for its successful drugs via new trials (extending use cases for Kisqali, Cosentyx, Scemblix, etc.).
- **Innovative trial designs** for new technologies (adaptive trials for radioligand therapies, basket trials potentially for gene/cell therapies in multiple diseases).
- **Geographical expansion** to integrate emerging markets (like China) fully into the development cycle.
- Leveraging **regulatory designations** (fast track, orphan) to expedite trials and approvals – e.g., many of Novartis’s pipeline assets like abelacimab (Fast Track for CAT), asciminib (was FDA fast-tracked for CML), iptacopan (various orphan designations) have special statuses to streamline development.

Novartis’s pipeline and trial portfolio thus show a clear trajectory: by focusing on core areas and high-value projects, the company aims to deliver a series of impactful medicines in the next 5 years. Each upcoming trial or data readout (pelacarsen, radioligands, remibrutinib, etc.) is geared towards that goal. If these bets pay off, Novartis will solidify its position in cardiology, oncology, immunology, and neuroscience for the foreseeable future, fulfilling its strategy of higher “value per new molecular entity”. The continuous cycle of trials – from concept to approval and life-cycle management – underpins Novartis’s R&D productivity and will be closely watched by the industry.
